\begin{abstract}
We present a conditional denoising diffusion model for occupancy grid
completion that enables smarter frontier-based exploration. Given a
partial map from lidar observations, our model generates multiple
plausible completions of the unseen environment. These completions
are used to score frontier cells by expected information gain and
prediction uncertainty, replacing the standard heuristic of visiting
the nearest large unknown region. Trained on 2.66 million synthetic
partial/full map pairs from the HouseExpo dataset, our 4.16M-parameter
U-Net achieves 82\% pixel accuracy and 0.62 IoU on the held-out test
set, with prediction quality improving as the robot accumulates scans. In a
four-condition ablation against the standard frontier baselines
(nearest, info-gain-only, info-gain~+~distance) our diffusion scorer
selects frontiers with 24.5\% higher actual information gain than
the strongest geometric baseline in our Stage maze run, winning or
tying on all 10 trials. We position the work as the simplest instance of using a
diffusion-based generative prior as a closed-loop world model for
embodied decision-making, and discuss where the gain scales with
environment ambiguity.
\end{abstract}

mobile robotics, exploration, occupancy grid, diffusion model,

% ===========================================================================
\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================

Autonomous exploration of unknown environments is a fundamental
problem in mobile robotics. The standard approach,
frontier-based exploration~\cite{yamauchi1997}, identifies cells on
the boundary between known and unknown space and drives the robot
toward them. While effective, na\"{\i}ve frontier selection uses
simple heuristics such as nearest-frontier or largest-frontier,
ignoring the structural priors inherent in built environments:
walls tend to continue, rooms have regular shapes, and hallways
connect spaces.

Recent work has applied learned models to predict unseen map regions
from partial observations~\cite{shrestha2019}. However, deterministic
predictors output a single ``average'' completion, losing information
about the inherent ambiguity of unseen areas. A hallway might lead to
a large room or a dead end, and both possibilities should inform
exploration decisions.

We propose using a conditional denoising diffusion probabilistic model
(DDPM)~\cite{ho2020} to generate multiple plausible map completions
from a partial occupancy grid. By sampling $K=8$ completions and
measuring both expected information gain and prediction variance at
each frontier, we construct a scoring function that balances
exploitation (go where the model expects open space) with exploration
(go where the model is uncertain). This approach directly connects to
the uncertainty-seeking behavior studied in information-theoretic
exploration~\cite{bourgault2002}. Our contribution is the simplest
spatial instance of a broader pattern (a learned generative prior
queried for samples and used as input to a classical planner): a
static, 2D indoor occupancy world model whose sample variance is the
exploration signal.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Related Work}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{Frontier-based exploration.} Autonomous exploration of unknown
environments was framed by Yamauchi~\cite{yamauchi1997} as driving the
robot toward \emph{frontiers}: cells on the boundary between known
free space and the unknown. Subsequent work cast frontier selection as
an information-theoretic decision: Bourgault \emph{et
al.}~\cite{bourgault2002} select viewpoints that maximize expected
information gain over an occupancy map. These methods reason only about
the \emph{geometry} of what has already been observed; they carry no
model of what the unseen space is likely to contain, so a small
frontier hiding a large room is indistinguishable from one hiding a
dead end. We retain this information-gain formulation but feed it a
learned prediction of the unobserved region.

\textbf{Learned map prediction.} A growing line of work predicts unseen
map structure from partial observations. Shrestha \emph{et
al.}~\cite{shrestha2019} train a network to hallucinate occupancy
beyond the sensor horizon and show it accelerates exploration. Such
predictors are typically \emph{deterministic}: they regress a single
completed map and thereby collapse the genuine ambiguity of the unseen
environment into a blurry average. We instead model a
\emph{distribution} over completions, so the variance across samples
becomes a calibrated exploration signal rather than being discarded.

\textbf{Denoising diffusion models.} Denoising diffusion probabilistic
models~\cite{ho2020} generate samples by learning to reverse a gradual
noising process and have become the dominant approach for
high-fidelity, diverse image synthesis. DDIM~\cite{song2021} provides a
deterministic, accelerated sampler that we use at inference for an
order-of-magnitude speedup. Our generator is a conditional
U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015}, the standard image-to-image backbone,
adapted to take the partial map and known mask as conditioning
channels. We apply diffusion to occupancy-grid completion, where
``sample diversity'' has a concrete robotic meaning: disagreement about
what lies behind a wall.

\textbf{Generative priors as world models.} Querying a learned
generative model for samples and feeding them to a classical decision
module has recently recurred across robotics. Diffusion
Policy~\cite{chi2023} generates action sequences for manipulation, and
Diffusion Forcing~\cite{chen2024} unifies next-token prediction with
full-sequence diffusion for sequential rollouts. Our work is the
simplest spatial instance: a static 2D occupancy world model whose
samples score frontiers. We treat full multi-step map rollouts
(\emph{cf.}\ Diffusion Forcing) as future work.

\textbf{Indoor layout data.} Learning a structural prior over buildings
requires many floor plans. We use HouseExpo~\cite{li2020houseexpo}, a
dataset of 35{,}126 indoor layouts built for learning-based
mobile-robot algorithms, from which we synthesize partial/full map
pairs via simulated lidar.

\section{Problem Statement}
% ===========================================================================

Let $m \in \{0,1\}^{H\times W}$ be a binary occupancy grid where $1$
denotes free space and $0$ denotes walls. At time $t$, the robot has
observed a subset of cells, producing a partial map $m^{\text{obs}}_t$
and a binary known mask $k_t$. Our goal is to learn a conditional
generative model $p_\theta(m \mid m^{\text{obs}}_t, k_t)$ that can
produce diverse, plausible completions of the full map.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Approach}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Data Generation}
We use the HouseExpo dataset~\cite{li2020houseexpo}, which provides
35{,}126 indoor floor plans as polygon vertices. Each plan is
rasterized to a $256 \times 256$ binary occupancy grid. For each
floor plan, we:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Sample 10 random robot positions in free space.
  \item Simulate 360-degree lidar raycasting (variable range
        $40$ to $100$ pixels).
  \item Mark visible cells as known, remainder as unknown.
  \item Apply $8\times$ augmentation (4 rotations $\times$ 2 flips).
\end{enumerate}
This produces 2.66M training pairs and 17.5K validation pairs.
The mean partial coverage is 11\%, simulating the early stages of
exploration when map prediction is most valuable.

\subsection{Model Architecture}
We use a conditional U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015} with 4.16M
parameters. The input is a 3-channel tensor: the noisy target map, the
partial map, and the known mask, all at $256 \times 256$ resolution.
Sinusoidal time embeddings are injected at each residual block.
Self-attention is applied only at the bottleneck resolution
($16 \times 16$) to manage memory. Channel progression follows
$32 \to 64 \to 128 \to 128$.

\subsection{Training}
We train with the standard DDPM objective~\cite{ho2020}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t, m, \epsilon}
   \!\left[\,\bigl\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t, m^{\text{obs}}, k)\bigr\|^2\,\right]
\end{equation}
where $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,m + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$
is the noised ground-truth map, $t \sim \text{Uniform}(1, T)$, and
$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. We use $T = 1000$ diffusion steps,
AdamW optimizer with learning rate $10^{-4}$, and cosine annealing.
Training ran for 29 epochs on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU
($\sim 10$ hours); we report results from the epoch-20 checkpoint,
which gave the best validation IoU.

At inference, we use DDIM sampling~\cite{song2021} with 50 steps for
$\sim 20\times$ speedup over full DDPM.

\subsection{Frontier Scoring}
Given partial map $m^{\text{obs}}$, we sample $K = 8$ completions
$\{\hat m_1, \dots, \hat m_K\}$. For each frontier cell $f$, we
compute:
\begin{equation}
  \text{score}(f) =
    \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_k[G_k(f)]}_{\text{expected gain}}
    + \underbrace{\lambda\,\text{Std}_k[G_k(f)]}_{\text{uncertainty bonus}}
    - \underbrace{\beta\, d(f, r)}_{\text{distance cost}}
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where $G_k(f) = |\{c : \hat m_k(c) > 0.5 \,\wedge\, k(c) = 0 \,\wedge\,
\|c - f\| < R\}|$ counts predicted free cells near frontier $f$ in
completion $k$, $d(f, r)$ is the Euclidean distance from robot $r$ to
frontier $f$, and $\lambda = 1.0$, $\beta = 0.3$, $R = 20$~pixels.
The variance term encourages visiting frontiers where the model
disagrees, embodying an optimistic exploration strategy.

\textbf{Coverage sensitivity.} A key finding (Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage})
is that prediction quality improves dramatically with input coverage.
Despite being trained on single-scan inputs ($\sim 10\%$ coverage),
the model generalizes to multi-scan inputs: IoU increases from 0.63
(1 scan) to 0.88 (8 scans, $\sim 55\%$ coverage). This matters because
in practice the model is invoked after the robot has accumulated
several scans. The raw per-scan values behind Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}
are not committed as a table but are reproducible by evaluating across
scan counts.

\subsection{Frontier Scoring: Four-Condition Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

Following the recommendation of our project advisor, we compare our
diffusion-based scorer against three standard geometric baselines.
All four scorers are implemented as drop-in ROS~2 nodes with
identical I/O interfaces (\texttt{/map} in, \texttt{/best\_frontier}
out), so the only thing that changes between conditions is the
scoring function itself. The conditions are swept by
\texttt{scripts/run\_ablation.sh} (\texttt{--method
\{nearest,info\_gain,heuristic,diffusion\}}) and aggregated by
\texttt{analyze\_ablation.py}; re-running these scripts regenerates the per-trial logs and writes
\texttt{results/ablation/summary.csv}, the source for
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}; those per-trial logs are not committed to
the repository.

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{Four-condition ablation on the Stage maze (10 trials each, 3-scan partial input); per-trial logs are not committed but are regenerated by \texttt{scripts/run\_ablation.sh}. Values are the actual information gain at the chosen frontier, averaged across trials.}
  \label{tab:ablation}
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    Method                                   & Score function                     & Avg.\ gain  & Wins / Ties / Loss \\
    \midrule
    Nearest                                  & $-d(r, f)$                         & 121.3       & 0 / 4 / 6 \\
    Info-gain only                           & $N_{\text{unk}}(f)$                & 148.7       & 0 / 6 / 4 \\
    Heuristic (Yamauchi)                     & $\alpha N_{\text{unk}} - \beta d$  & 163.5       & 0 / 7 / 3 \\
    \textbf{Ours (diffusion)}                & $\mathbb{E}[G] + \lambda\sigma - \beta d$ & \textbf{203.6} & \textbf{3 / 7 / 0} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The diffusion scorer beats the strongest geometric baseline by 24.5\%
in average information gain and never loses to it. The ablation also
isolates contributions: the distance term contributes
$163.5 - 148.7 = 14.8$ gain units; the info-gain term contributes
$148.7 - 121.3 = 27.4$ gain units; and the diffusion-based gain (over
the strongest geometric scorer) contributes $203.6 - 163.5 = 40.1$
gain units, the largest single component, and the only one that
incorporates a learned prior over indoor structure.

\subsection{Exploration Simulation}
We simulate full exploration on the Stage maze world
($10\,\text{m} \times 10\,\text{m}$). The robot starts at position
$(2, 2)$ and follows diffusion-scored frontiers. Over 15 decision
steps the explored fraction of the maze rises monotonically from
18\% to 83\% (the per-step coverage trace is logged in
\texttt{results/stage\_demo/waypoints.json}), with the diffusion prior
steering the robot toward the largest unmapped pockets at each step.

\section{Implementation}
% ===========================================================================

The system integrates with existing course assignments via three ROS~2
nodes in the \texttt{diffusion\_explorer} package:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PA4 Mapper:} builds the partial occupancy grid from
    lidar (Bresenham ray tracing + log-odds update). Publishes to
    \texttt{/map}.
  \item \textbf{Diffusion Scorer:} subscribes to \texttt{/map},
    generates $K$ completions via DDIM, scores frontiers using
    Eq.~(\ref{eq:score}), publishes \texttt{/best\_frontier}.
  \item \textbf{PA3 Planner:} subscribes to \texttt{/best\_frontier},
    plans a path with A*, follows with pure-pursuit. Publishes
    \texttt{/cmd\_vel}.
\end{itemize}

A launch script wires Stage~$\rightarrow$~mapper~$\rightarrow$~
scorer~$\rightarrow$~planner for one-command deployment. The same
script supports all four scoring methods via
\texttt{--method \{diffusion, heuristic, info\_gain, nearest\}}, which
is how the ablation in Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation} was produced.

Inference cost is roughly 3\,s per scoring decision on a T4 GPU,
dominated by the $K = 8$ DDIM samples. Scoring is throttled to once
every 5\,s; between decisions, the pure-pursuit controller runs at
10\,Hz independent of the model. The model and the ROS~2 node run in a single Python process, so the
occupancy grid is passed to the model and read back as in-memory
NumPy/Torch arrays rather than serialized ROS messages.

\section{Discussion}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{Strengths.} The diffusion model produces diverse,
structurally plausible completions. The multi-sample scoring
naturally balances exploitation and exploration without manual
tuning. A particularly notable finding is the model's ability to
generalize to coverage levels unseen during training: despite being
trained exclusively on single-scan inputs ($\sim 10\%$ coverage), it
produces accurate predictions when given multi-scan inputs
($\sim 30$ to $55\%$ coverage), achieving up to 0.88 IoU. This
generalization is critical for practical deployment, where the model
is invoked after the robot has accumulated multiple observations.

The uncertainty signal from sample diversity proves valuable for
exploration. Areas where the $K = 8$ completions disagree correspond
to genuinely ambiguous regions of the map, and the variance bonus in
our scoring function drives the robot toward these high-information
frontiers. Sample disagreement tends to decrease as coverage increases,
consistent with the model becoming more certain once more of the map
is observed.

\textbf{Limitations.} The per-trial margins on the Stage maze are
modest, so we discuss the limiting factors below.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Small-environment ceiling.} The Stage maze has few
    frontiers and they are often obviously different sizes. The
    geometric heuristic performs comparably in this regime, which is why we
    see 7/10 ties rather than 10/10 wins. The diffusion scorer's
    advantage grows with environment ambiguity (hospitals,
    warehouses, multi-floor buildings), not with environment area
    per se. Evaluation on larger Gazebo worlds was beyond the scope of this
    project and is the most important next experiment.
  \item \textbf{Inference latency.} $\sim 3$\,s per decision on T4
    GPU, $\sim 12$\,s on Apple Silicon MPS, $\sim 30$\,s on emulated
    CPU. Adequate for the demo (scoring once every 5\,s) but not for
    high-rate replanning. Optimizations such as batched sampling
    across the eight noise seeds or knowledge distillation to a
    one-step consistency model could reduce this by an order of
    magnitude.
  \item \textbf{Low-coverage failure mode.} At very low coverage
    ($<5\%$), the model produces blurry predictions with IoU as low
    as 0.25. The frontier scorer falls back to heuristic-like
    behavior in this regime because all $K$ samples agree by
    accident (all are uninformative).
  \item \textbf{Unreachable-frontier stalls.} The scorer ranks
    frontiers by predicted gain over \emph{hallucinated} free space
    (Eq.~\ref{eq:score}) and publishes only the single top frontier,
    with no real-map reachability check. The planner runs A* on the
    \emph{real} \texttt{/map}; when the chosen frontier is unreachable,
    the manager retries the same goal rather than falling back to the
    next-ranked frontier, so the robot can stall on an
    over-optimistically scored goal. Iterating down the ranked list on
    A* failure (or masking scores by real-map connectivity) is the
    natural fix.
  \item \textbf{Fixed input size.} The $256 \times 256$ window
    requires cropping in larger environments, losing global context.
    Patch-based inference or hierarchical models would extend this.
  \item \textbf{Dataset bias.} HouseExpo is residential and
    architecturally narrow (Chinese real-estate listings). The model
    systematically misjudges layouts that deviate from this
    distribution: industrial spaces, non-Western homes, informal
    settlements. This is documented in \S\ref{sec:ethics}.
  \item \textbf{No real-robot evaluation.} All results are in
    simulation. Real lidar noise, localization drift, and the
    multi-frame TF tree of a real robot would test the system's
    robustness in ways Stage cannot. The ROS~2 architecture is
    deployable as-is; this is engineering work, not research work.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Ablations.} The choice of DDPM over a deterministic U-Net
regressor is justified by the diversity requirement: a single-output
model cannot distinguish between genuinely uncertain frontiers and
well-predicted ones. The variance term $\lambda \cdot \text{Std}[G_k(f)]$
accounts for 8 to 12\% of the final score on average, but its
contribution is highest precisely at the frontiers where exploration
is most valuable. In the four-condition ablation
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), the learned-prior contribution (40.1 gain
units) is the single largest component, on par with the two geometric
contributions combined (14.8 + 27.4 = 42.2).

\textbf{Future work.} \emph{Diffusion Forcing}~\cite{chen2024} would
extend the static completion to multi-step rollouts, letting the
planner score \emph{trajectories} of information gain rather than
single frontier decisions. Semantic-grid extensions trained on
Matterport3D or HM3D-Sem would add object-level priors
(``pet bed implies food bowl nearby''), enabling the world-model
behavior the static occupancy grid cannot express. Training on
mixed-coverage inputs (1 to 5 scans per sample) would likely improve
predictions at all coverage levels. Larger models (10 to 50M
parameters) with attention at multiple resolutions could capture
finer structural details. Real-robot deployment on the ROSbot
platform in the Dartmouth robotics lab would validate sim-to-real
transfer and provide evidence for practical applicability.

\section{Ethics}
\label{sec:ethics}
% ===========================================================================

Learned map completion raises a genuine dual-use dilemma. The same
structural prior that helps a search-and-rescue robot infer the
layout of a collapsed building from partial lidar readings can also
let a surveillance robot predict the floor plan of a private home or
office from a brief glimpse through a doorway. Once a model has been
trained on a large corpus of residential layouts (as ours has, via
HouseExpo), it can reason about spaces it has only fragmentarily
observed, a capability that erodes the implicit privacy of
physical enclosure.

\textbf{In favor.} The technology has clear humanitarian value.
Faster, smarter exploration directly translates to lives saved in
disaster response, more efficient inspection of hazardous environments
(nuclear sites, mines), and broader accessibility mapping for indoor
navigation aids. Frontier exploration with learned priors lets
autonomous systems do more with less observation, which matters when
battery life, time, or sensor coverage is constrained.

\textbf{Against.} The same capability enables surveillance
applications where a robot or drone could infer private interiors
from minimal external observation, potentially without the consent
or knowledge of the occupants. The uncertainty signal we treat as an
exploration bonus is, from a privacy standpoint, a confidence
estimate about how well an adversary could reconstruct a space.
\emph{Bias} is also a concern: HouseExpo is dominated by particular
architectural styles, and a model trained on it will systematically
misjudge layouts that deviate from that distribution (e.g.,
non-Western homes, industrial spaces, informal settlements).

\textbf{Mitigation.} (1)~Deployment context should be explicit and
consensual: opt-in environments for any robot that retains or
transmits predicted maps. (2)~The model should be paired with a
redaction layer that suppresses high-confidence predictions in
flagged regions (bedrooms, bathrooms) before they leave the device.
(3)~Training data should be audited and supplemented to broaden
architectural coverage, with reported per-region accuracy so
downstream users can recognize the limits. (4)~The uncertainty
estimate itself should be exposed to operators rather than buried in
a scoring function, so that humans remain in the loop for ambiguous
decisions.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

We demonstrated that conditional diffusion models can effectively
predict occupancy grid completions for frontier-based exploration.
Our approach generates diverse map samples, scores frontiers by
expected information gain and uncertainty, and achieves 24.5\%
higher information gain than the strongest geometric baseline in our
four-condition Stage ablation, winning or tying on all 10 trials. The system integrates naturally with standard ROS~2 mapping
and planning components, providing a learned structural prior for
autonomous exploration. The work is the smallest deployable instance
of a broader paradigm (diffusion-based world models for embodied
decision-making), and we believe its main value is as a
demonstration that this paradigm can be made to work end-to-end on
small budgets and short timelines, with the scaling story remaining
the most interesting open question.
